# The Turning Point
## Three days across the valley where a rebellion became a revolution

### Overview

On October 17, 1777, British General John Burgoyne surrendered 5,895 soldiers to American General Horatio Gates in a field outside Saratoga — the first time an entire British army had ever capitulated. Within months, France recognized the United States, and what had been a colonial revolt became a global war. But Saratoga wasn't a single event. It was the collapse of an entire strategy — three British armies converging on the Lake Champlain–Hudson River corridor to split the colonies in two — and it unraveled across 200 miles of Upstate New York in a single brutal summer. This three-day itinerary follows that unraveling, from the political drawing rooms of Albany to the wilderness fortifications at Ticonderoga, and asks at every stop: how did one valley, one summer, and one surrender change the world?

### Before You Go

Start in Albany. The route moves west into the Mohawk Valley on Day 2, then north to Lake Champlain on Day 3, ending at Ticonderoga — plan a return drive of roughly three hours to Albany, or continue north into the Adirondacks. Best season: late September through mid-October, when the Saratoga anniversary and peak foliage coincide.

## Day 1: Albany and Saratoga — The Political Architecture of a Revolution

In the winter of 1777, Philip Schuyler sat in his Albany mansion coordinating the defense of the northern frontier while his political enemies in Congress tried to replace him. His daughter Eliza would marry Alexander Hamilton in the same house three years later. Today's route begins where the revolution's northern strategy was built — in drawing rooms, not trenches — and ends on the field where it was won.

---

**Schuyler Mansion State Historic Site**
Albany, Albany County · 1.5 hours

Philip Schuyler was the Continental Army's second-in-command in the north, a logistics genius who organized the retreat from Ticonderoga and the scorched-earth campaign that slowed Burgoyne's advance to a crawl. Congress relieved him of field command weeks before Saratoga — and gave the victory to Gates. The Georgian mansion Schuyler built in 1765 survived a Loyalist raid in 1781 (the hatchet marks are still in the front hall railing) and hosted Washington, Franklin, and the wedding of Hamilton and Eliza Schuyler in December 1780.

*Don't miss:* the hatchet marks on the front staircase railing — scars from the 1781 Loyalist raid that attempted to kidnap Schuyler and nearly succeeded.

*Practical:* Mid-May through October, Wed–Sun. Free admission. 32 Catherine Street, Albany. nysparks.com

---

**Saratoga National Historical Park — Battlefield**
Stillwater, Saratoga County · 2.5 hours

Between September 19 and October 7, 1777, two battles on these fields — Freeman's Farm and Bemis Heights — destroyed Burgoyne's army. The American victory wasn't inevitable. At Freeman's Farm, Daniel Morgan's riflemen broke the British center but couldn't hold it. Three weeks later, Benedict Arnold — officially relieved of command, possibly drunk, certainly furious — charged into the second battle on horseback, rallied the American left, and took a musket ball in his leg that shattered his femur. His charge broke the British line. His leg saved the revolution. His name would become the most infamous in American history within three years.

*Don't miss:* the Boot Monument at Stop 7 on the tour road — a carved bas-relief of a military boot honoring "the most brilliant soldier of the Continental Army." Arnold's name is deliberately left off the pedestal. The blank space where it should be says more than any inscription.

*Practical:* Battlefield tour road open daily Apr–Nov, 9 AM–6 PM; visitor center year-round, 9 AM–5 PM. Free admission (fee-free NPS unit). 648 Route 32, Stillwater. nps.gov/sara. US250 2026: Victory Season Living History Weekend Oct 10–11; Turning Point Symposium May 2; recreation of Knox's Noble Train of Artillery; July 4th celebration at Saratoga Racetrack. Full schedule at saratoga250.com.

---

**Saratoga National Historical Park — Schuyler House**
Schuylerville, Saratoga County · 45 minutes

After Burgoyne retreated north from the battlefield, he burned Schuyler's country estate on the way. Schuyler rebuilt it — this house — within a year, a declaration that the Schuylers weren't leaving. The site sits a few miles from the surrender field where Burgoyne's army laid down their arms. The house is the private counterpart to the battlefield: the personal cost of the revolution to one of its architects.

*Don't miss:* the view from the back of the house toward the Hudson — the same view Schuyler saw when he chose to rebuild on scorched ground.

*Practical:* Open weekends, Memorial Day through Labor Day. Free admission. Route 4, Schuylerville. nps.gov/sara

---

**Saratoga Surrender Site**
Schuylerville, Saratoga County · 30 minutes

On October 17, 1777, Burgoyne's 5,895 men laid down their arms in this field along the Hudson — the first time an entire British army had surrendered in history. The 19-acre site is quiet now. A granite wall faces the river, and a bronze bas-relief reproduces John Trumbull's painting *The Surrender of General Burgoyne* — the original hangs in the U.S. Capitol Rotunda. The landscape hasn't changed much since 1777. Stand at the plaza overlooking the Hudson and you're seeing essentially the same view the surrendering army saw.

*Don't miss:* the bronze bas-relief of Trumbull's *Surrender of General Burgoyne* — then compare it to the original next time you're in the Capitol.

*Practical:* Grounds open dawn to dusk, year-round. Free. Route 4, half mile south of Schuylerville. nps.gov/sara

---

**Saratoga Monument**
Schuylerville, Saratoga County · 30 minutes

The 155-foot obelisk in the center of Schuylerville has four niches — one for each of the American generals at Saratoga. Three hold statues: Schuyler, Gates, Morgan. The fourth niche — Arnold's — is empty. It's been empty since the monument was dedicated in 1883. You can climb the interior staircase to the top for a view across the Hudson Valley that maps the entire campaign.

*Don't miss:* the empty fourth niche. Stand in front of it. Consider what it means that a nation would carve a space for a hero it couldn't name.

*Practical:* Grounds open year-round; interior open seasonally. Free. 1 Burgoyne Street, Schuylerville.

---

**Stay:** Saratoga Arms (497 Broadway, Saratoga Springs) — a restored 1870 brick hotel two blocks from Congress Park, where the proportions of the rooms remind you that the Gilded Age built things to last. Independent, locally owned.

**Eat:** Hattie's Restaurant (45 Phila Street, Saratoga Springs) — Southern-inflected comfort food that's been a Saratoga institution since 1938. The fried chicken has survived every trend the town has thrown at it.

## Day 2: The Mohawk Valley — Where the Western Arm Broke

On the morning of August 6, 1777, a relief column of 800 Oneida warriors and Tryon County militia walked into an ambush in a ravine west of the village of Oriskany. General Nicholas Herkimer took a musket ball through the knee early in the fight. He had his men prop him against a beech tree, lit his pipe, and directed the battle for six hours while bleeding out. Half his column was dead or wounded by nightfall. The Haudenosaunee Confederacy — intact for centuries — fractured along lines that never fully healed, as Oneida fought Seneca and Mohawk in what was effectively a civil war. Today's route covers the ground where the western arm of the British strategy died, and asks what it cost.

---

**Fort Stanwix National Monument**
Rome, Oneida County · 2 hours

In August 1777, Colonel Peter Gansevoort held this frontier fort with 550 men against a British force three times his size for 21 days. Barry St. Leger's western column — the arm of the three-pronged attack meant to join Burgoyne at Albany — stalled here and never advanced again. The NPS has reconstructed the fort to its 1758 appearance; uniformed rangers walk the bastions and know the siege hour by hour. But the fort's significance stretches beyond 1777: the 1768 Treaty of Fort Stanwix, signed here, ceded vast Haudenosaunee territories and set the stage for every conflict that followed.

*Don't miss:* the 1768 Treaty of Fort Stanwix exhibit — the document that redrew the boundary between colonial settlement and Haudenosaunee territory, and the chain of consequences that hasn't ended.

*Practical:* Tue–Sat, 9 AM–5 PM (closed Sun–Mon). Free admission. 112 East Park Street, Rome. 315-338-7730. nps.gov/fost

---

**Oriskany Battlefield State Historic Site**
Oriskany, Oneida County · 1 hour

The bloodiest battle of the Revolutionary War by casualty rate happened here, in a ravine beside Oriskany Creek. General Herkimer's relief column, marching to break the siege at Fort Stanwix, was ambushed by Loyalists and Haudenosaunee allies of the British under Joseph Brant. The fighting was hand-to-hand. Neighbors killed neighbors. Oneida warriors fought Mohawk warriors. When the smoke cleared, the Haudenosaunee Confederacy's unity — the foundation of its power for centuries — was broken. The monument on the hill marks where Herkimer sat under his tree, smoking his pipe, refusing to leave.

*Don't miss:* the Herkimer monument at the center of the site — and then stand at the edge of the ravine where the ambush began. The terrain explains the battle better than any exhibit.

*Practical:* Mid-May through mid-Oct; Wed–Sat, 9 AM–5 PM; Sun, 1–5 PM. Free admission. 7801 State Route 69, Oriskany. parks.ny.gov

---

**Herkimer Home State Historic Site**
Little Falls, Herkimer County · 1 hour

Nicholas Herkimer was carried from Oriskany to this house on the Mohawk River, where a Continental Army surgeon amputated his shattered leg ten days after the battle. The amputation went wrong. Herkimer bled to death on August 16, 1777, reportedly reading his Bible aloud in German — his first language — to his family as he died. The house is a fine Georgian-style brick home in a county of frame farmhouses, a reminder that Herkimer was the wealthiest man in the valley and chose to march anyway. Little Falls (population 4,500) is a Mohawk Valley mill town that rewards a slow drive through.

*Don't miss:* the room where Herkimer died — the family Bible and the bed placement are based on period accounts. The quietness of the room against the violence of the story is the point.

*Practical:* Mid-May through mid-Oct; Wed–Sat, 10 AM–5 PM; Sun, 1–5 PM. Tours hourly from 10 AM (last at 4 PM). Adults $4, seniors/students $3, under 12 free; grounds free. 200 State Route 169, Little Falls. 315-823-0398. parks.ny.gov

---

**Old Stone Fort Museum**
Schoharie, Schoharie County · 1 hour

Schoharie is a county seat of 1,200 people. The Old Stone Fort Museum on the edge of town tells a story you'll find nowhere else: the Palatine German migration of 1710, when 3,000 refugees from the Rhineland settled the Schoharie and Mohawk Valleys and became the backbone of the frontier militia that fought at Oriskany. The building itself — a 1772 Reformed church fortified during the Revolution — survived a 1780 raid by Joseph Brant and Sir John Johnson. The collection inside is local, personal, and irreplaceable: ledgers, tools, family Bibles in German script.

*Don't miss:* the Palatine German genealogy collection — if your family came through the Schoharie Valley, the records here go back to 1710.

*Practical:* May–Oct, Tue–Sat, 10 AM–5 PM. Adults $7. 145 Fort Road, Schoharie. theoldstonefort.org

---

**Stay:** Inn at Stone Mill (4 Keyser Avenue, Little Falls) — a converted 19th-century mill on the Mohawk River, where the industrial architecture and the river sound through the windows remind you what powered this valley before electricity.

**Eat:** Canal Side Inn (395 South Ann Street, Little Falls) — a canalside restaurant in a town that the Erie Canal made and the railroads nearly unmade. The Friday fish fry is what people drive to Little Falls for.

## Day 3: Lake Champlain — The Corridor That Started Everything

On the night of May 10, 1775, Ethan Allen and 83 Green Mountain Boys crossed Lake Champlain in the dark, climbed the walls of Fort Ticonderoga, and captured the British garrison in what one soldier called "a confused melee of shouting." It was America's first offensive military victory — and it mattered less for the symbolism than for the 59 cannons Henry Knox would drag 300 miles on ox sleds to Boston the following winter. Those cannons ended the siege of Boston. This day retraces the corridor — the Lake Champlain–Hudson River gap — that every army in colonial America understood was the key to the continent.

---

**Crown Point State Historic Site**
Crown Point, Essex County · 1.5 hours

Two days after Allen took Ticonderoga, Seth Warner captured Crown Point — and 111 more cannons. The ruins here are older and larger than Ticonderoga's: the massive stone walls of the 1759 British fort and the remains of the earlier French Fort St. Frederic form one of the most impressive military landscapes in North America. Almost nobody comes here. Crown Point (population 2,000) sits on a limestone promontory jutting into Lake Champlain, and on a clear day the view north reaches into Vermont and Canada. This is an RTN destination — a place that exists on its own terms, not as a sidebar to somewhere else.

*Don't miss:* the ruins of Fort St. Frederic — the French fortification that preceded the British fort. The scale of the walls, crumbling into the lake, is the most dramatic physical remnant of the colonial wars in New York.

*Practical:* Grounds open May–Oct, Wed–Sun, 9:30 AM–5 PM. Free admission (parking fee in season). 739 Bridge Road, Crown Point. nysparks.com

---

**Fort Ticonderoga**
Ticonderoga, Essex County · 3 hours

Ethan Allen arrived at these walls with 83 men and no artillery plan. He left with the cannons that ended the siege of Boston — 59 pieces of ordnance that Henry Knox dragged on ox sleds across 300 miles of frozen New England in January 1776. The fort itself has been restored to its 18th-century appearance by the Mars family (yes, the candy) since 1908, and it's the most thoroughly interpreted colonial military site in the country. The King's Garden, between the fort and the lake, is one of the most beautiful cultivated spaces in the Adirondacks. Fort Ticonderoga's REAL TIME REVOLUTION program — immersive, multi-year, and running through 2027 — is built specifically for the 250th anniversary.

*Don't miss:* the view from the ramparts looking north across Lake Champlain to Mount Defiance — the high ground the British seized in July 1777 to force the American evacuation. Stand on the wall and understand why control of this point meant control of the continent.

*Practical:* Open daily May–Oct, 9:30 AM–5 PM. Adults $26. 102 Fort Ti Road, Ticonderoga. fortticonderoga.org. US250 2026: REAL TIME REVOLUTION programming all season — immersive living history events tied to the 250th anniversary. Book ahead.

---

**Hancock House**
Ticonderoga, Essex County · 30 minutes

The Ticonderoga Historical Society operates this 1926 replica of John Hancock's Boston home — a peculiar and specific piece of local pride. The collection inside focuses on the town's industrial and military history: the pencil-lead mines, the paper mills, the fort's role across three wars. It's a local museum doing exactly what local museums should do — preserving what the national sites don't.

*Don't miss:* the collection of Ticonderoga-branded pencil memorabilia — yes, the pencils were named after the town, not the other way around.

*Practical:* Open seasonally; check ticonderogahistoricalsociety.org for hours. Free admission. 6 Moses Circle, Ticonderoga.

---

**Stay:** The Burgoyne (1 Hudson Street, Schuylerville) — named, with local irony, for the general who surrendered here. A recently renovated boutique inn on the Hudson, independent and rooted in the town's identity.

**Eat:** The Hot Biscuit Diner (14 Montcalm Street, Ticonderoga) — a genuine Adirondack diner where the portions are built for people who work outside. The biscuits are what the name promises.

---

### Closing Note

Drive south from Ticonderoga along Route 22 and the lake opens to your left — Vermont's Green Mountains on the opposite shore, the same view Ethan Allen saw when he crossed in the dark. Every cannon that rolled out of these forts in the winter of 1775–76 traveled south, then east, then arrived on Dorchester Heights above Boston Harbor, and the British fleet sailed away. Burgoyne came back through this same corridor two years later, certain he could finish what Gage had started. He surrendered in a field outside Saratoga with 5,895 men, and France recognized a country that didn't yet have a constitution. The valley hasn't changed much. The forts are still here. The questions they raise — about what's worth defending, and what it costs — haven't been answered. They've just been inherited.
