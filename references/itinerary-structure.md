# Itinerary Structure — Templates and Rules

## Document Architecture

```
[TITLE]
[Subtitle — the arc in one evocative line]

OVERVIEW
[Narrative hook. Opens with a scene or specific historical moment.
States the through-line question. Does NOT list the stops. 3–4 sentences.]

BEFORE YOU GO
[Logistics. Starting point. Driving notes. Season. 2–3 sentences max.]

DAY [N]: [Title]
[Narrative note — 2–3 sentences. Opens with a person or moment. States what the day answers.]

  [STOP BLOCKS]

  Stay: [Named property — 1 sentence with specific detail]
  Eat: [Named restaurant — 1 sentence with a specific dish or reason]

[CLOSING NOTE]
[Returns to the opening image. Does not summarize. Does not invite booking.
Ends with an observation, image, or question that earns the journey.]
```

## Day Title Formula

`Day [N]: [Specific Place or Person] — [What It Means]`

✓ "Day 1: Ticonderoga — The Cannons That Ended a Siege"
✓ "Day 2: Oriskany and Rome — Where the Confederacy Broke"
✓ "Day 3: Saratoga — The Surrender That Changed Everything"
✓ "Day 1: Auburn — The Life Harriet Tubman Actually Lived"
✓ "Day 2: Seneca Falls — Seven Words That Took 72 Years"

✗ "Day 1: Exploring the Capital Region"
✗ "Day 2: Revolutionary War Sites"
✗ "Day 1: History and Culture of the Finger Lakes"

---

## Narrative Note Formula (Opens Each Day)

Three sentences. Never more.

1. **Specific scene or person** — name a person, put reader somewhere, name a date or moment
2. **The stakes** — what mattered about what happened here
3. **The question this day asks** — what the visitor should be wondering as they drive

**Example (Arc 1, Day 2):**
*On the morning of August 6, 1777, a relief column of 800 Oneida warriors and Tryon County
militia walked into an ambush in a gorge west of the village of Oriskany. Half were dead
or wounded within five hours. The Haudenosaunee Confederacy — intact for centuries —
fractured along lines that never fully healed. Today's route covers the ground where that
fracture happened, and asks what it cost both sides.*

## Stop Block Template

```
**[Site Name]**
[City, County] · [Time to allow]

[2–4 sentences. Opens with a scene, person, or specific fact — NOT the founding date.
What happened here. What makes this stop necessary to the arc. Named specifics.]

*Don't miss:* [One hyper-specific thing — the object, the room, the view, the document]

*Practical:* [Hours; admission; 2026 US250 programming if confirmed; website]
```

**Rules:**
- Never open with "Located in..." or "This [type] offers..."
- "Don't Miss" must be a named specific, not a category
- Practical information is always last
- 2–4 sentences maximum in the main body; discipline is everything

**Opening line patterns that work:**
- "In [month year], [person] [did something specific]..."
- "The [object/room/view] here is the only [thing] in [scope]..."
- "[Person] [action] — and [consequence]..."
- "When [person] [arrived/left/died/surrendered] in [year]..."

**Opening line patterns that don't:**
- "Located in [town], this [type of site]..."
- "Step back in time at..."
- "A must-see for history buffs..."
- "[Site] is one of the most important..."

## "Don't Miss" Examples

✓ "Don't miss: the Boot Monument — a carved bas-relief of a boot honoring Benedict Arnold's charge, with his name deliberately left off the pedestal."
✓ "Don't miss: the seven-door ground floor of the Hasbrouck House — a Dutch colonial quirk Washington's staff found disorienting and practical in equal measure."
✓ "Don't miss: the longhouse interior — the domestic scale of Haudenosaunee life, reconstructed from archaeological sources, suddenly makes the civilization present."
✓ "Don't miss: the weighlock chamber, where canal boats were floated in, weighed, and taxed — the last surviving room of its kind in the country."
✓ "Don't miss: the original buckboard wagon from TR's midnight ride, in the Adirondack Experience's main hall."

## Day-Planning Rules

**Stops per day:** 3–5. Never more. Heritage sites require time.
**Time estimates:**
- Major NPS / State Historic Site: 1.5–2 hours
- Smaller museum or lesser-known site: 1 hour
- Monument, landscape, memorial: 30 minutes
- Build in one "slow" stop per day — unscheduled, unhurried

**Driving legs:** No more than 90 minutes between stops. Map the route before writing.
If it's more than 90 minutes, either reroute or flag it as a deliberate scenic drive.

**Mix rule:** Each day must include at least one nationally significant site AND at least
one lesser-known site. Do not stack famous sites all on one day.

## Lodging and Dining Rules

### Lodging
Always name a specific property. Never write "find accommodation in [town]."
Rural or independently owned properties preferred (RTN mission).

✓ "Stay at the Saratoga Arms (Broadway, Saratoga Springs) — a restored 1870 brick hotel
   two blocks from Congress Park, where the Gilded Age still lingers in the proportions."
✓ "Stay at the Inn at 22 Montgomery (Seneca Falls) — a Victorian B&B four blocks from
   the Women's Rights NHP, family-run since 1998."
✗ "There are many lodging options in the area."

### Dining
Lead with food specificity. Name a dish. Connect to local food culture where possible.

✓ "Eat at Springside Inn (Auburn) — the beef tenderloin has been on the menu since 1919."
✓ "Stop at Gem Diner (Herkimer) — the kind of place where the soup is from last night's roast."
✗ "There are many restaurants in the area to suit all tastes."

## RTN Mission Integration

The Rural Tourism Network's mission is to promote rural destinations as worthy in their own
right — not as scenic backdrops for urban visitors. Every itinerary must:

- Include at least one stop in a town with population under 5,000
- Name that town explicitly and frame it as the destination
- Not apologize for rurality or frame it as "off the beaten path"
- Celebrate the specific thing that exists only in that place

**Good RTN framing:**
"Schoharie is a county seat of 1,200 people. The Old Stone Fort Museum on the edge of town
tells a story — the Palatine German migration of 1710 — that you will find nowhere else."

**Bad RTN framing:**
"If you have extra time, a short detour to the charming small town of Schoharie offers
a hidden gem for history buffs."
